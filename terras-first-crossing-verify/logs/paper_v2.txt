                                                Abstract
          Terras’s coefficient stopping time conjecture asks whether the shortcut Collatz orbit of
      every integer n ≥ 2 lies below n when its coefficient ρk = 3d /2k first falls below one. Here
      k counts shortcut steps and d counts their odd inputs. We prove that every eligible realizer
      descends when the first-crossing word has at most 75 maximal runs of odd inputs. Any
      counterexample requires at least 76 such runs, 10 439 860 591 steps and 6 586 818 670 odd
      inputs. The proof uses a lower bound on the seed from the length of its odd runs, rational
      approximations to log2 3, the verified range of Rozier and Terracol, and Rhin’s estimate
      for a linear form in logarithms. We also prove descent for a family with arbitrarily many
      odd runs. Two further restrictions narrow the remaining words. An initial square U U in
      a failing word satisfies |U | < 35 log2 k. For a counterexample of shortest crossing length,
      the reciprocal-sum estimate of Nikzad, Frinkle and Bagherzadeh gives a lower bound on
      ρk whose value begins 0.9441733669 . . .. The open inequality compares the least eligible
      starting value in each remaining parity class with an explicit threshold.


1    What is being asked
Write T (n) = (3n + 1)/2 at odd inputs and T (n) = n/2 at even inputs. Fix n ≥ 2. For
the parity word w of its first k input states n, T (n), . . . , T k−1 (n), let d count the odd inputs.
Induction gives
                                     2k T k (n) = 3d n + Cw ,                                      (1)
where the nonnegative integer Cw depends only on w. Write ρk = 3d /2k with ρ0 = 1. An odd
step multiplies the coefficient by 3/2 and an even step halves it. Let τ (n) be the first k with
ρk < 1, and let χ(n) be the first k with T k (n) < n. An empty defining set gives stopping time
∞. Equation (1) gives τ (n) ≤ χ(n), so any failure of Terras’s conjecture τ (n) = χ(n) has finite
k = τ (n) and T k (n) ≥ n [14, 4]. An even starting value descends at its first step, so a failure
has odd n and d ≥ 1.
    A first-crossing word has ρj ≥ 1 for every j < k and ρk < 1. Its last step is even, so
ρk ∈ [1/2, 1). With d ≥ 1, this also gives 2k−1 ≤ 3d < 2k and k = ⌈d log2 3⌉ ≤ 2d. An odd run
is a maximal block of consecutive odd input states.

Remark 2 (Testing all eligible realizers of a word). A length-k parity word specifies one residue
class modulo 2k . Indeed, the two lifts of a given length-j class modulo 2j+1 have starting values
that differ by 2j . Their images after j steps differ by 3dj , an odd integer, so exactly one lift
has the prescribed next parity. For a first-crossing word, let r(w) be the least integer ≥ 2 in
its class. Every eligible realizer descends if and only if r(w)(ρ−1k − 1) > Mw , since the left side
increases with the seed. If a seed n fails, its endpoint satisfies 0 ≤ T k (n) − n < s by Proposition
1 and (3).
Remark 3 (What is classical here). Almost all of the above is in Lagarias’s survey [9, §2.2–§2.3],
and the reader should not take any of it as new.
    The residue-class correspondence is Terras [14, Thm. 1.2, Cor. 1.3] and is Theorem B of
[9]; Garner [2, §4] gives it in the coefficient-stopping-time form, modulo 21+p(m) . Lagarias’s
equations (2.4)–(2.6) are Proposition 1: his T (k) (n) = λk (n)n + ρk (n) has λk = ρk in our
notation and ρk (v) = Cw /2k , so that Mw = ρk (v)/λk . His (2.13)–(2.14) then state that the
elements of a class with
                               n > ε−1k ρk (v),     εk = 1 − λk (v),
have ω(n) = σ(n), and rearranging that inequality gives n(ρ−1        k −1) > Mw exactly. The descent
criterion above is therefore Lagarias’s, not ours.
     Sharper still, Theorem E of [9] states that there is an effectively computable k0 such that for
admissible v of length k ≥ k0 , every element of S(v) except possibly the smallest has stopping
time k. Its proof is the one we use: a linear forms in logarithms bound (there Baker–Feldman,
|k log 2 − l log 3| ≥ k −c0 ; here Rhin’s k −13.3 ), fed into (2.14). “Only the least element of the
class can fail” is thus Theorem E, asymptotically in k.
     What the present section adds is only this. The criterion is stated with no “sufficiently large”
and no ineffective k0 : it is a single monotone comparison valid at every k, so one evaluation
at r(w) decides the class outright. And a failure is pinned to the s endpoints n, . . . , n + s − 1.
The quantitative work is in §3, where the run decomposition (2) replaces Lagarias’s cruder
admissibility estimate ρk (v) ≤ k2(1−θ)k and makes k0 explicit.

plus one equals (3/2)ℓ (x+1), so 2ℓ | x+1. At each odd step x+1 is multiplied by 3/2, and at each
even step it decreases. Writing Di for the number of odd steps before run i and L = log2 (n + 1),
we get 2ℓi ≤ (n + 1)(3/2)Di , hence Di+1 ≤ L + αDi with α = log2 3, and therefore

    Neither ingredient is new. The divisibility 2ℓ | x + 1 across a run of ℓ odd steps appears
explicitly as Lemma 8 of Hercher [3], written there as 1 ≤ ki ≤ xi with xi = log2 (ni + 1);
that is the antecedent we are aware of. Garner’s contribution is separate and is recorded
below: the coefficient stopping time itself [2, §4] and the Diophantine study of powers of 2
against powers of 3 [2, §5–§6]. The geometric sum Gs = (αs − 1)/(α − 1) is likewise the sum
(δ m2 − 1)/(δ − 1) appearing in Hercher’s Theorem 21, with the same constant δ = log2 3 = α;
his recurrence xi+1 < δxi is the purely geometric form that cyclicity permits, while the affine
form Di+1 ≤ L + αDi above re-injects the seed size L at every run because a finite path has a
fixed starting point.
    The method of the next theorem — a Diophantine bound on powers of 2 against powers of
3, combined with a finite verification range — is Garner’s [2, §6–§7]; see Remark 8.

Theorem 6. Every eligible realizer of a first-crossing word with at most 75 odd runs descends
at that crossing. Any counterexample to Terras’s conjecture has at least 76 odd runs, crossing
time k ≥ 10 439 860 591 and odd-input count d ≥ 6 586 818 670.

Remark 7 (Dependencies of Theorem 6). The Rozier–Terracol verification supplies the seed
bound n > B. Combined with (4), the Farey interval then gives the two integer bounds. Rhin’s
estimate enters only in the case d ≥ 261 ; its hypothesis is H = max(|u1 |, |u2 |) ≥ 2 [12, Prop. 6.3],
and with u1 = k, u2 = −d and k > d one has H = k, so the form used is Λ ≥ k −13.3 .
Remark 8 (Historical context, and what is claimed here). Every method used above is older
than this note; what is new is the combination and the resulting explicit constants for finite
first crossings.
    Garner [2] is the direct precedent. He defined the coefficient stopping time, also formulated
the conjectured equality with the stopping time — he cites Terras [14] as his reference [3] —
and verified it for n < 1 150 000, identified the realizing residue class modulo 21+p(m) with
p(m) = ⌊m log2 3⌋, and proved (his §6) that σ(n) = κ(n) whenever the number of odd terms m
is below a bound assembled from a study of the powers of 2 and 3 — his tabulated breakpoints
are denominators of convergents of log2 3 — together with a verified range. His §7 applies this
to cycles, obtaining at least 35 400 terms from n > 6 · 107 . Theorem 6 is that argument with
two changes: the hypothesis bounds the number of odd runs rather than odd terms, which is
strictly weaker because a single run may contain arbitrarily many odd steps, and the modern
verification range and an explicit Farey sandwich replace his tabulated constants.
    Simons and de Weger [13] developed the run-growth approach for nontrivial cycles, bound-
ing the number of local minima; Hercher [3] strengthened that line to m ≥ 92. Their counts
and ours are the same kind of quantity but not the same quantity: in a cycle the odd runs close
up, so the number of local minima equals the number of runs, whereas in a finite first-crossing
word the first run may begin at the seed, which is not a local minimum, and the last run is
cut off by the crossing. Their bounds also use cyclicity, through the closing of the product
around the cycle, which a finite path does not provide. The numerical agreement between the
threshold 76 here and the Simons–de Weger value m ≥ 76 is a coincidence of similar inputs:
with a modern verification range their cycle bound becomes m ≥ 83 [3], while ours stays at 76.
Nothing in this note raises the first-crossing threshold to 92, and Hercher’s cycle
conclusion must not be read as applying to finite first crossings.
    Lagarias [9] is the closest predecessor of all, and Remark 3 gives the locators. His (2.14)
is our descent criterion verbatim after rearrangement, and his Theorem E already proves, by
exactly our route — linear forms in logarithms fed into that criterion — that for admissible
words of length k ≥ k0 only the smallest element of the class can fail. What is left to us is to
make k0 explicit under a hypothesis on the run count, which is what Lemma 5 and Theorem 6 do.
Lagarias also records [9, §2.3] that Terras [14] and “later Garner” [2] conjectured the equality,
which settles the question of independence raised above.
    Rozier and Terracol [12] supply the verification range and, in their Remark 5.5, credit
Simons with proposing classification by local maxima. Three classes of object must be kept
apart. A nontrivial cycle is the subject of [2, 13, 3]. A paradoxical sequence [12, Def. 1.1]
is a finite trajectory with T j (n) ≥ n and coefficient below one at step j, for any such j. A
first-crossing failure, the object here, is a paradoxical sequence with j = τ (n), hence a proper
subclass. Their Theorem 5.3 excludes paradoxical sequences with 93 ≤ j ≤ 301 993 outright,
and all with 4615 ≤ n ≤ 2.8 · 1019 ; the 301 993 is the extent of a finite computation, whereas
the bound k ≥ 10 439 860 591 of Theorem 6 is a lower bound on a counterexample derived from
the Farey sandwich. A counterexample must satisfy both, and ours is the stronger constraint;
there is no conflict.

4    A family with arbitrarily many runs
Proposition 9. Repeat the block 110 exactly h ≥ 1 times and append zeros up to the first
coefficient crossing. Every eligible realizer descends at that crossing.

Proof. One block acts by H(x) + 5 = 9(x + 5)/8, so −5 is its fixed point. The block’s full
coefficient is 9/8 and its two nonempty proper-prefix coefficients are 3/2 and 9/4. Consequently
the first crossing occurs among the appended zeros at k = ⌈h log2 9⌉ ≤ 4h. Iteration gives
H h (n) + 5 = 9h (n + 5)/8h . Integrality implies 8h | n + 5, hence n ≥ 8h − 5. The normalized
memory is 5(1 − (8/9)h ) < 5 and the appended zeros leave it unchanged.
    For h ≤ 63, k ≤ 252 is below the crossing-length bound in Theorem 6. For h ≥ 64, Rhin’s
estimate gives nΛ ≥ (8h − 5)/(4h)13.3 > 5. Indeed, 864 − 5 > 5 · 25614 , and ((h + 1)/h)14 ≤
(65/64)14 < 8, so 8h − 5 > 5(4h)14 persists by induction. A failure requires nΛ < n(eΛ − 1) ≤
Mw < 5, a contradiction.

   The prefix (110)h and the completed word generally have different least seeds. Write m =
k − 3h and n = 8h t − 5. All these n form the binary prefix class, since 23h = 8h is its exact
modulus. Its least positive representative is 8h −5. To append m zeros we also need 2m | 9h t−5,
the block endpoint. At h = 12 this gives k = 39, m = 3 and t ≡ 5 (mod 8). The prefix and
completed-word least seeds are therefore 68 719 476 731 and 343 597 383 675, respectively.

                                2ℓ ≤ x − n < x + 1 ≤ (3/2)ℓ (n + 1).

approximation results in [5] concern infinite parity words.

7    The open question
A shortest counterexample would have at least 76 odd runs, obey the coefficient bound in
Proposition 11, and have no initial square whose period exceeds the bound in Proposition 10.
For each remaining first-crossing word w, its coefficient and its additive correction are known
exactly. The open estimate for its least eligible realizer r(w) is

                                              Cw         Mw
                                   r(w) >          =    −1    .
                                            2 − 3d
                                             k
                                                       ρk − 1

Equality already gives a counterexample. A lower bound for r(w) or an upper bound for the
right side can certify a word if it preserves this strict inequality. The rational-shadow estimates
of [6] supply bounds for individual blocks. Applying one to this question requires transporting
it back to the starting seed and comparing it with the threshold of the actual first-crossing
prefix.

Status of the results
It is worth separating three things explicitly.
    Established before this note. The residue-class correspondence for parity words (Terras [14]);
the affine form, the descent criterion n > ε−1
                                             k ρk (v), and — in Theorem E — the statement that
for admissible words of length k ≥ k0 only the smallest element of a class can fail, proved
from linear forms in logarithms (Lagarias [9, §2.2–§2.3, Thm. E]); the coefficient stopping time,
the conjecture κ = σ, and the method of combining a Diophantine bound on 2a against 3b
with a finite verification range, including a descent theorem under a bound on the number of
odd terms (Garner [2]); run-growth bounds for the number of local minima in a nontrivial
cycle (Simons–de Weger [13], Hercher [3]); verification of t = τ to 2.8 · 1019 and the exclusion of
paradoxical sequences with 93 ≤ j ≤ 301 993 (Rozier–Terracol [12]); the reciprocal-sum estimate
(Nikzad–Frinkle–Bagherzadeh [10]).
    Offered here. The transfer of Garner’s method to finite first crossings with the hypothesis
weakened from odd terms to odd runs, which is what Lemma 5 buys; the resulting explicit
constants k ≥ 10 439 860 591, d ≥ 6 586 818 670 and a threshold of 76 runs; descent for the
whole repeated-110 family, which has arbitrarily many runs; the finite-word square bound |U | <
35 log2 k; the effective and non-asymptotic form of Lagarias’s Theorem E that these constants
provide; and the reciprocal-sum restriction ρk > 0.9441733669 on a shortest counterexample.
Subject to the priority caveat of Remark 8.
    Unresolved. A lower bound on the least positive realizer of a many-run word with a shallow
crossing and no long initial repetition — the inequality of §7 — is open. Whether Hercher’s cycle
techniques transfer to finite first-crossing trajectories, and in particular whether the threshold
of 76 runs can be raised, is left for future work; no such raising is attempted or claimed
here. Terras’s conjecture itself is open, as is the Collatz conjecture.


Reproducibility
The companion verifier checks the numerical inequalities used in the proofs, recomputes the
finite bracket count, and verifies the worked examples using integer and rational arithmetic.
It lives in the companion repository github.com/innerlightr-wq/terras-first-crossing,
with the verification code pinned at the immutable commit a93b1e8. Run the following com-
mand from the project root.

python3 scripts/verify_crossing.py

The script checks the numerical inequalities with integers and rationals, encloses ln 2 and ln 3
by a 96-term series with a geometric tail, recomputes the finite bracket sum in (164) of [10],
and encloses each exponential cutoff by alternating Taylor bounds. The SHA-256 digest of the
verifier begins f852c88311071a0d. The orbit and residue-class examples are finite checks. The
proofs for arbitrary word lengths are in the text.
    The finite seed verification of Rozier–Terracol, Rhin’s estimate, the high-path accounting
of Nikzad–Frinkle–Bagherzadeh and the height record cited above are external inputs. The
general case of Terras’s conjecture remains open.
