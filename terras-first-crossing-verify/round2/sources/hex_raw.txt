Abstract
Terras’s coefficient stopping time conjecture asks whether the shortcut Collatz orbit of
every integer n ≥ 2 lies below n when its coefficient ρk = 3d /2k first falls below one. Here
k counts shortcut steps and d counts their odd inputs. We prove that every eligible realizer
descends when the first-crossing word has at most 81 maximal runs of odd inputs. Any
counterexample requires at least 82 such runs, 10 439 860 591 steps and 6 586 818 670 odd
inputs. The proof uses a lower bound on the seed from the length of its odd runs, rational
approximations to log2 3, the verified range of Rozier and Terracol, and Rhin’s estimate
for a linear form in logarithms. The run bound is applied not once but at several interior
starts, selected by the size of their contributions to the correction term. We also prove
descent for a family with arbitrarily many odd runs. Two further restrictions narrow the
remaining words. An initial square U U in a failing word satisfies |U | < 35 log2 k. For a
counterexample of shortest crossing length, the reciprocal-sum estimate of Nikzad, Frinkle
and Bagherzadeh gives a lower bound on ρk whose value begins 0.9441733669 . . .. The open
inequality compares the least eligible starting value in each remaining parity class with an
explicit threshold.

What the present section adds is only this. The criterion is stated with no “sufficiently large”
and no threshold k0 : it is a single monotone comparison valid at every k, so one evaluation at

threshold on the number of steps k, whereas our hypothesis bounds the number of runs, and his
conclusion exempts the smallest element of the class while ours covers it. The two statements
are therefore distinct. What we do make explicit, in Corollary 9, is the exemption itself.

The next corollary concerns all words, with no hypothesis on the run count. It is the effective
form of the exemption in Lagarias’s Theorem E: that theorem leaves the smallest element of
a class unresolved for k ≥ k0 , with k0 effectively computable but not evaluated there, and the
statement below removes the length restriction altogether.

Proof. Let n fail at crossing length k. By Proposition 1 and (3), nΛ < n(eΛ − 1) ≤ Mw ≤ d/3,
and Rhin’s estimate gives Λ ≥ k −13.3 , so n < (d/3)k 13.3 ≤ k 14.3 /3. A length-k parity word

Remark 10 (Dependencies of Theorem 8). The Rozier–Terracol verification supplies the seed
bound n > B. Combined with (6), the Farey interval then gives the two integer bounds. Rhin’s
estimate enters only in the case d ≥ 265 ; its hypothesis is H = max(|u1 |, |u2 |) ≥ 2 [13, Prop. 6.3],

these, the proof of Theorem 8 uses only Proposition 1 with (2) and (3), Lemma 5, and certified
enclosures of ln 2 and ln 3. It uses no formalised or externally computed bound on individual
runs; in particular it is independent of the two-run bounds discussed in Remark 11. Whether
81 is optimal for this method is open: the thresholds depend on the available convergents and
on the choice of r in each row, and we have not searched that space exhaustively.
Remark 11 (Historical context, and what is claimed here). Every method used above is older
than this note; what is new is the combination and the resulting explicit constants for finite
first crossings.
Garner [3] is the direct precedent. He defined the coefficient stopping time, also formulated
the conjectured equality with the stopping time — he cites Terras [15] as his reference [3] —
and verified it for n < 1 150 000, identified the realizing residue class modulo 21+p(m) with
p(m) = ⌊m log2 3⌋, and proved (his §6) that σ(n) = κ(n) whenever the number of odd terms m
is below a bound assembled from a study of the powers of 2 and 3 — his tabulated breakpoints
are denominators of convergents of log2 3 — together with a verified range. His §7 applies this
to cycles, obtaining at least 35 400 terms from n > 6 · 107 . Theorem 8 is that argument with
two changes: the hypothesis bounds the number of odd runs rather than odd terms, which is
strictly weaker because a single run may contain arbitrarily many odd steps, and the modern
verification range and an explicit Farey sandwich replace his tabulated constants.
Simons and de Weger [14] developed the run-growth approach for nontrivial cycles, bounding the number of local minima; Hercher [4] strengthened that line to m ≥ 92. Their counts and
ours are the same kind of quantity but not the same quantity: in a cycle the odd runs close up,
so the number of local minima equals the number of runs, whereas in a finite first-crossing word
the first run may begin at the seed, which is not a local minimum, and the path stops instead
of closing. The last odd run is complete: were step k odd we would have 2k−1 ≤ 3d−1 and
2k > 3d , whence 3d < 2k ≤ 2 · 3d−1 < 3d . The crossing therefore occurs in the even block that
follows the last run, as the proof of Lemma 7 uses. Their bounds also use cyclicity, through the
closing of the product around the cycle, which a finite path does not provide. With a modern
verification range their cycle bound becomes m ≥ 83 [4].
A different route to a run threshold proceeds from a bound on the first two runs of every
small seed. Justin Alick has formalised such local two-run bounds in Lean 4 [1], among them
the declaration allPairs70, which establishes ℓ1 + ℓ2 ≤ 76 for every odd n < 270 and is sharp
at n = 806 134 805 793 934 253 823 with runs of lengths 8 and 68. Combined with the present
estimates that bound yields a threshold of 76 runs. The proof of Theorem 8 does not use it, and
the two routes are independent. Nothing in this note raises the first-crossing threshold
to 92, and Hercher’s cycle conclusion must not be read as applying to finite first
crossings.
Lagarias [10] is the closest predecessor of all, and Remark 3 gives the locators. His (2.14)
is our descent criterion verbatim after rearrangement, and his Theorem E already proves, by
exactly our route — linear forms in logarithms fed into that criterion — that for admissible

under a hypothesis on the run count rather than on k. Lagarias also records [10, §2.3] that Terras
[15] and “later Garner” [3] conjectured the equality, which settles the question of independence

Rozier and Terracol [13] supply the verification range and, in their Remark 5.5, credit
Simons with proposing classification by local maxima. Three classes of object must be kept
apart. A nontrivial cycle is the subject of [3, 14, 4]. A paradoxical sequence [13, Def. 1.1]
is a finite trajectory with T j (n) ≥ n and coefficient below one at step j, for any such j. A
first-crossing failure, the object here, is a paradoxical sequence with j = τ (n), hence a proper
subclass. Their Theorem 5.3 excludes paradoxical sequences with 93 ≤ j ≤ 301 993 outright,
and all with 4615 ≤ n ≤ 2.8 · 1019 ; the 301 993 is the extent of a finite computation, whereas
the bound k ≥ 10 439 860 591 of Theorem 8 is a lower bound on a counterexample derived from
the Farey sandwich. A counterexample must satisfy both, and ours is the stronger constraint;
there is no conflict.
Hikawa [5] reviews the problem and singles out exactly the triple studied here — the correction term qv , the coefficient difference 2k − 3d , and the least positive representative rv of the
residue class. Priority for the specific first-crossing statements of this note is therefore not established; we have not located a match, which is not the same as there being
none.

Status of the results
It is worth separating three things explicitly.
Established before this note. The residue-class correspondence for parity words (Terras [15]);
the affine form, the descent criterion n > ε−1
k ρk (v), and — in Theorem E — the statement that
for admissible words of length k ≥ k0 only the smallest element of a class can fail, proved from
linear forms in logarithms (Lagarias [10, §2.2–§2.3, Thm. E]); the coefficient stopping time,
the conjecture κ = σ, and the method of combining a Diophantine bound on 2a against 3b
with a finite verification range, including a descent theorem under a bound on the number of
odd terms (Garner [3]); run-growth bounds for the number of local minima in a nontrivial
cycle (Simons–de Weger [14], Hercher [4]); verification of t = τ to 2.8 · 1019 and the exclusion of
paradoxical sequences with 93 ≤ j ≤ 301 993 (Rozier–Terracol [13]); the reciprocal-sum estimate
(Nikzad–Frinkle–Bagherzadeh [11]).
Offered here. The transfer of Garner’s method to finite first crossings with the hypothesis
weakened from odd terms to odd runs, which is what Lemma 5 buys; the resulting explicit
constants k ≥ 10 439 860 591, d ≥ 6 586 818 670 and a threshold of 82 runs; the splitting and
restart lemmas, which apply the run bound at several interior starts rather than once from
the seed; descent for the whole repeated-110 family, which has arbitrarily many runs; the
finite-word square bound |U | < 35 log2 k; Corollary 9, which removes the length restriction
from the conclusion that only the least eligible seed can fail; and the reciprocal-sum restriction
ρk > 0.9441733669 on a shortest counterexample. Subject to the priority caveat of Remark 11.
Unresolved. Combining Theorem 8 with Corollary 9, what remains is exactly this: the
least eligible seed of a first-crossing word with 82 or more odd runs. A lower bound
on that seed for a word with a shallow crossing and no long initial repetition — the inequality
of §7 — is open. Whether 81 is optimal for the present method is also open; the thresholds
depend on the available convergents and on the choice of r in each row, and that space has not
been searched exhaustively. Whether Hercher’s cycle techniques transfer to finite first-crossing
trajectories is a separate question, not addressed here. Terras’s conjecture itself is open, as is
the Collatz conjecture.
